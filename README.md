# Introduction to Agricultural Informatics

These learning materials introduce students to current and emerging digital technologies with applications in agriculture, environment, and food systems. Through a modular hands-on course design, students will incrementally learn user-centered technology design skills, computational thinking skills to evaluate, design, conceptualize, and implement informatics solutions to data-driven challenges. Students will gain programming skills in current software development technology stacks (Python, HTML, CSS, SQL, Django), with an emphasis on web applications. Students will design an informatics solution to problem in their own area of interest. This course supports professional development for students in pursuit of careers in digital product design or data-driven research.

**For students new to computing:** These materials provide digital-novices with a gentle on-ramp to learn digital agriculture skills so that they can better understand how technology works and is built. While you may have a deeper understanding of the unique challenges of your domain, you will learn how to engage in domain-driven **software development**. Through these materials, students will gain the vocabulary and experience to be able to identify advanced courses for more in-depth learning.

**For students with a more significant computing background:** These materials are designed to provide domain-novices with a breadth-first exploration of digital agriculture, so that they can apply their skills to develop technology for agricultural, environmental, and food systems applications. While you may be comfortable with computational work, you will learn how to engage in **domain-driven** software development. Through these materials, students will be able to identify and develop appropriate software solutions to address agriculture, environment, and food systems research and practice.

## Learning Objectives

These materials support the following types of learning objectives:

1. Demonstrate core skills in user-centered technology design, exploratory data analysis, and programming for the web.
2. Formulate a data science problem and implement a data science solution through collecting, integrating, analyzing, and synthesizing publicly available data
3. Critique and experiment with informatics approaches to solving data-driven challenges in disciplines related agriculture, environment, and food systems.
4. Design and develop a web application through application of new knowledge on data modeling, user interface design, and programming.
5. Design and conceptualize a novel informatics solution, applying design thinking and computational skills, to solve an open problem related to agriculture, environment, and food systems.

## AI Policy (for Learners)

I'm giving this edition of Intro to Ag Informatics the tagline: *"from pseudocode to vibecode"* because of the following notion:

The purpose of this course is to teach you how to read and write code. I will support you in thinking critically about technology and prepare you to debate what constitutes good digital design practices. But, this course is also designed to teach you about emerging technologies, and that includes, the use of contemporary AI technologies, such as LLMs. Thus, we must thread the needle of responsible AI use carefully.

In writing, ideating, and critical thinking: do your own work, don't let anyone or anything reason on your behalf. But you are welcome to ask questions, discuss, consult. Write your own thoughts, build out your ideas, and credit the foundations you build upon: whether AI, your peer's advice, or a reference material.

For writing code, a little different recommendation: 

* In modules 1, 2, 3 (web development, data exploration, data models):
    * Do not use any AI tools to generate code. It defeats the purpose of learning HTML, CSS, Python, and SQL. 
    * Hand-crafting code will build up your fundamental coding skills.
* In modules 3, 4, and 5 (data models, web application, user interfaces):
    * Only use AI tools to explore and debug your code - to learn how to improve.
    * First, write your pseudocode, your logic by hand.
    * Second, in your code editor (e.g., VScode): outline your code as comments, write "function designs". These are your coding prompts for YOU.
    * Third, use VScode and language "cheatsheets" (one-page language summaries) to translate your thinking to code. That's programming!
    * Finally, when you're stuck and receive error messages, use a search tool, check Stack Overflow, or use an LLM to ask thoughtful questions to improve your own learning. Debugging Django can be tough - use these tools especially to learn how to sift through your code and find bugs in code snippets.
* In module 6 (software engineering):
    * We'll use AI tools to vibecode, while learning how to write and use good software specifications.

In all cases, disclose if and how you used AI tools in your work. When in doubt, ask. But also: save and/or include transcripts or sample prompts and responses as evidence.

## When and why do I create new materials vs curate supplementary materials?
When I was in graduate school I came across the computer science education book, [Nand to Tetris](https://www.nand2tetris.org/). It teaches people how to "build a modern computer from first principles", and readers traverse: boolean logic, micro-electronics, computer architecture, and move up the stack of languages and abstractions learning how we go from electronic circuits (NAND) to video games (Tetris). 

The Nand to Tetris book taught me that it is possible to teach people how systems work from first principles, but it also demonstrated the breadth of content and subdisciplines that learners must traverse, while demonstrating how to weave around a laundry-list of pre-requisites.

> Lesson #1: Computing from first principles is a vast journey, but begins with gaining comfort with technical vocabulary, knowing where to find support, practicing computational and critical thinking, and learning how to design SYSTEMS iteratively. It's less about learning EVERYTHING and more about learning how to learn.

I taught a LOT during my time in grad school across the [School of Information and Computer Sciences at the University of California, Irvine](https://ics.uci.edu/). These experiences ranged from TAing software design and video game development courses for [informatics](https://informatics.ics.uci.edu/explore/) undergrads and grads alike, to teaching an introduction to programming course for non-computer science majors. Prior to that, I co-instructed a type of digital literacy course while living in Fiji and studying at the [University of South Pacific](https://www.usp.ac.fj/), which began with naming computer peripherals (monitor, mouse, keyboard) to using spreadsheets.  

> Lesson# 2: The digital divide persists across continents and within disciplines. Each student comes with a different level of literacy, including digital literacy and has varied experiences with technology, including digital technologies. If each learners' topography of experiences is unique, then it's less about teaching everyone everything, and more about learning how to identify and fill your knowledge and skills gaps. 

Maybe one day these materials will become a book or something else.

But in the meantime, I needed to curate materials that (1) help a variety of learners to identify and fill knowledge and skills gaps, while navigating the dynamic and vast expanse of interdisciplinary concepts. 

> Consequence for this course: I reference a broad range of supplementary materials to allow learners to fill gaps when you need to, without forcing everyone down the same fundamentals. If you find a useful learning material, please share: https://github.com/ag-informatics/ag-informatics-course/discussions

This gives me space to focus my energy on creating useful and domain-driven content that I cannot find easily in existing educational materials -- hence this repository.
